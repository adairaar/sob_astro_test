# Feature B: classification of all in-corpus ἵστημι passages

Every in-corpus occurrence read in context (3 lines either side) from TLG,
exact wordform search, Exact Match + Diacritics sensitive, 19 authors,
Aristotle restricted to Meteorologica {0086.026}, Theophrastus to De signis
{0093.008}.

## Result

**121 in-corpus occurrences. 0 B-positive.**

No text in the corpus uses ἵστημι of a celestial body coming to rest above a
named terrestrial location. If the feature is scored on ἵστημι,
*p* = 1/(121+1) = **0.0082**, against 0.056 recorded for the στηρίζω proxy.

**Matthew's own form ἐστάθη: 0 occurrences in the corpus.**

## ⚠ The finding that cuts against us

**ἵστημι *is* the technical verb for a planetary station.** The SI currently
argues the opposite (§"Available alternative vocabulary for ἵστημι"), claiming
the standard term is στηρίζω "not ἵστημι". Five passages falsify that:

- **Geminus, *Elem. astr.* 1.20** — "…sometimes to move faster, sometimes
  slower, sometimes **to stand still** (ἑστηκέναι) — which indeed they call
  **στηριγμούς** in the case of the five planets." An explicit equation.
- **Theon, *De util. math.* 148.2** — the formal definition: "στηριγμός is the
  appearance of a planet **standing still** (ἑστῶτος) and remaining for a
  longer time beside one of the fixed stars."
- **Paul of Alexandria 31.25–27** — "from the stars **standing still**
  (ἑστάναι) and going backwards… it was called στηριγμὸς ἀφαιρετικός"; and the
  second station likewise "from **standing still** and adding".
- **Theon 191.2** — the planet "will seem **to stand still** (ἑστάναι) and to
  go backwards", in a sentence that then names στηριγμοὺς καὶ ἀναποδισμούς.
- **Hipparchus, *Frag. geogr.* 7** — glosses μονάς ("stations") as ἵστασθαι.

So the lexical argument against Molnar as currently written is wrong and a
referee who checks will find it. **That paragraph has to be rewritten.**

### But the corpus result survives, and is sharper

The station sense of ἵστημι is real, and its complement is *always* a
celestial or geometric one — a zodiacal sign, a degree, an aspect, a chart
angle, a fixed star. Never a house, city, or person. So:

- Molnar gains a lexical possibility: ἐστάθη *could* be a station verb.
- Molnar loses the construction: no author in the corpus writes the station
  with a terrestrial complement, and Matthew's ἐπάνω οὗ ἦν τὸ παιδίον is
  exactly that.

The weight of the argument therefore shifts off the verb and onto **Feature C**
(ἐπάνω + named terrestrial point). That is where it should have been resting.

## The five hardest passages

Each is a near miss and each should be named in the SI, because a referee will
find them and it is better to have dealt with them first.

1. **Cleomedes, *Caelestia* 2.1.271** — "Syene lies under Cancer. Whenever the
   sun, coming to be in this sign, **stands** (στῇ) at exact noon, the things
   lit by it *in this region* become shadowless." A celestial body standing,
   and a named terrestrial city. **Negative**: Syene is tied to Cancer by
   κεῖται, not to the sun by ἵστημι; στῇ means reaching the meridian.
2. **Theon 132.26** — the zodiac circle "standing now this way now that
   **above us**" (ἱστάμενος ὑπὲρ ἡμᾶς). **Negative**: the subject is a circle,
   "us" is observers generally, and it describes orientation, not cessation.
3. **Manetho 6.287** — "whenever Phainon [Saturn] **stands above** the midmost
   centre" (ὑπὲρ κέντρου μεσάτοιο ἱστῆται). **Negative**: κέντρον is the chart
   angle, not a place on earth.
4. **Vettius Valens 2.32** — "the sign **in which the Moon stands**"
   (ἐφ᾽ οὗ ἕστηκεν ἡ Σελήνη), repeated for the Sun and Venus. Matthew's exact
   frame. **Negative**: the complement is always a ζῴδιον.
5. **Aratus 865** (= Achilles Tatius 19.31) — the Moon "**standing** straight
   between earth and sun" in eclipse. **Negative**: a geometric alignment with
   the whole earth, not a named place.

Also worth citing as a clean negative: **Vettius Valens 6.9.7**, "**setting
the Moon upon the degree**" (στήσας τὴν Σελήνην ἐπὶ τὴν μοῖραν) — ἵστημι +
celestial body + ἐπί, with a degree as the complement.

## Recommended wording for the result

Not "ἵστημι is not the astronomical term" — that is false. Rather:

> Greek technical astronomy does use ἵστημι of a celestial body ceasing its
> motion, and names the resulting event στηριγμός. In 121 occurrences across
> nineteen authors, the complement of that standing is invariably a zodiacal
> sign, a degree, an aspect, a chart angle, or a fixed star. It is never a
> terrestrial location. Matthew's construction — a star standing ἐπάνω the
> place where a child was — has no parallel in the corpus.
